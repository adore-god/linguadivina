The Court & The Creation

Strong's Exhaustive Concordance — Reference Source

Strong's Exhaustive Concordance of the Bible, first published in 1890 and compiled by the biblical scholar James Strong, is used on Lingua Divina as a secondary reference source for Hebrew and Greek word analysis. It is not the primary interpretive lens, but it provides the lexical grounding that supports accurate reading of key terms in the original languages. This page also shows where it is brief, so that every claim made from it on this site can be checked against the entry itself.

What Strong's Concordance Is

Strong's is an exhaustive index of every word appearing in the King James Version of the Bible. Each word is assigned a reference number corresponding to an entry in either the Hebrew and Aramaic lexicon for the Old Testament or the Greek lexicon for the New Testament. Those entries give the original word, its number, a short definition and the English words the King James translators used, allowing a reader to move from the English translation back to the original word and its semantic field.

How It Is Used on This Site

The linguistic framework on Lingua Divina depends on reading certain biblical terms with precision. Words such as Elohim, YHVH, and Ehyeh carry meanings that English translations flatten or obscure. Strong's entries are consulted alongside other available translations to establish what the original term actually encodes before any interpretive claim is made.

For example, the plural form of Elohim, the root of YHVH as the existing or self-existing one, and the first-person form Ehyeh meaning I AM or I will be, are all details that Strong's lexicon makes available directly. These distinctions are not incidental. They are the foundation on which the Framework & Articles A — Z and the broader Lingua Divina framework rest.

What a Strong's Entry Gives, and What It Does Not

A Strong's entry gives the number, the original word, a short definition and a list of the English words the King James translators chose for it. That makes it an excellent index: every verse where the word is used can be found from the number. As a dictionary it is brief. The definitions are written around how the King James Version translated each word, so an entry records what translators did as well as what the word is.

An entry does not give the reason for its labels, and it does not mark which verses are disputed. Those are found in the fuller lexicons, Brown–Driver–Briggs for Hebrew and Thayer's for Greek. For that reason Lingua Divina reads Strong's to find the word and its number, then reads the fuller entry before any interpretive claim is made.

Elohim (H430): The Test Case

Strong's calls Elohim the plural of H433, which it gives as probably prolonged from El (H410). The entry opens with "gods in the ordinary sense". It then says the word is "specifically" used of "the supreme God", applied "by way of deference to magistrates", and sometimes as a superlative. The English renderings it lists include "judges". The word is plural in form, and the entry says so.

The words "supreme God" are Strong's own, and the entry gives no reason for them. The root El is given as "strength" and "mighty", and Brown–Driver–Briggs opens the root with "god, god-like one, mighty one". Nothing in the form of the word says "supreme". That sense describes how the word is used and translated. In the fuller lexicon the first sense of Elohim is the plural one: "rulers, judges". The sense of one Deity is filed later, under a label the editors call "plural intensive", and under the senses headed "the (true) God" and "God". "Plural intensive" explains a plural form with a singular meaning. It is the editors' explanation of the form, and the entry itself marks several verses as disputed.

The English translation hides the word further. The King James Version has the English word "God" 2,346 times for Elohim, "god" 244 times, and "judge" 5 times. The reader is shown the word that translators chose, and the plural court that the Hebrew form names is not visible unless the entry is read.

The Verb and the One Court

The usual reason for reading the plural word as one is that the verb is singular. In Genesis 1:1 the verb bara (created, H1254) is third person singular. In Genesis 20:13 the verb hit'u (caused to wander, H8582) is third person plural, and in Genesis 35:7 nigl'u (appeared, H1540) is third person plural. The King James Version reads singular in all three, so the plural verb does not show in the English.

The same movement between plural and singular occurs inside Genesis 1:26-27. In the Bible in Basic English the verses read "Let us make man in our image, like us" and then "in the image of God he made him: male and female he made them". The court speaks as "us", then acts as "he", and the man made in its image is "him", then "them". On this site that is read as the court, the plural Elohim, made of many voices and acting as one, which is why the singular and the plural verbs both appear. This is the reading applied in the Elohim article. The entries and the verbs above are the part that can be checked, and the reading is Lingua Divina's own.

The Same Root in the Names

The root El (H410) is also built into Bible names, and Strong's gives each entry's parts. Daniel (H1840) is "judge of God", from Dan (H1835) and El, with Brown–Driver–Briggs giving "El is my judge". Elijah (H452) is "God of Jehovah", from El and Yah (H3050), glossed "my God is Jehovah". Joel (H3100) is "Jehovah (is his) God", from the name of YHVH (H3068) and El, and Strong's says it is the name of twelve Israelites. The same two parts, the root of Elohim and the name of YHVH, are built into the names of many different people in the narrative. Lingua Divina reads names as compressed identity codes, set out in the article on man made in the image, and Strong's is how the parts of each name are checked.

Strong's, BDB and Thayer's

Strong's is one of three old books that free Bible websites show. Brown–Driver–Briggs (1906) is the fuller Hebrew lexicon and Thayer's Greek-English Lexicon (1889) is the Greek one. Blue Letter Bible says on its own help page that the Online Bible, which supplied its Strong's data, changed Thayer's definitions on the Holy Spirit and the divinity of Christ, so the Thayer's text on that site is Thayer's with edits. The same help page says page images of the original lexicons have been added, so the original wording can be read.

What Websites Add

Some sites add a short summary line or commentary around the dictionary entry. On the Bible Hub page for Elohim the summary line reads "God, gods, divine beings, judges", and no author is named for it. That list is not in the Strong's entry. Its Topical Lexicon says the plural fits the later teaching of a triune God and that the word most frequently takes singular verbs for the one true God. Neither claim is in the Strong's, NAS or Brown–Driver–Briggs blocks on the same page. The check on every site is the same: go to the dictionary line, not the summary or commentary around it.

How to Check Any Word

Find the Strong's number for the word in the verse. Read the Strong's line, then the Brown–Driver–Briggs or Thayer's entry, with the numbered senses in order and the notes second. Count how the King James Version translated the word, which Blue Letter Bible lists, so that the English words that hide the senses can be seen. Read the verse in the Bible in Basic English with the chapter around it. Where a plural word is involved, check the verb in an interlinear, which gives its person and number. Then see whether the narrative follows the meaning, since a meaning that is right shows in how the passage turns out.

Its Scope and Limits

Strong's Concordance catalogues and defines. It does not interpret. The interpretive framework applied on this site, reading scripture as a map of consciousness mechanics, is original to Lingua Divina and is not derived from Strong's. The concordance is used to verify that the words being interpreted mean what the analysis claims they mean. Where Strong's entries are referenced in an article, they serve as lexical confirmation rather than argument.

Most quotations from scripture on Lingua Divina use the Bible in Basic English translation. To cross-check the original terms those quotations translate, Strong's references can be found by opening up quoted, in-article Bible passages and selecting Strong's mechanics.
