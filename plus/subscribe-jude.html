The Court & The Creation

Jude — The Court Reads Its Own Case Files

Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and the brother of James, to those of God's selection who have been made holy by God the Father and are kept safe for Jesus Christ: May mercy and peace and love be increased in you. — Jude 1:1–2

Jude names himself first by function — servant — then by relation: brother of James, whose own name carries "supplanter," the heel-catcher who becomes Israel only once the court finishes its work on him. Jude writes to people already described in three completed-tense verbs: sanctified, called, kept. The legal status is settled before the letter's argument even opens. What follows is not a new charge but a docket — case after case pulled from the court's own record, precedent stacked on precedent, showing what Elohim has always enforced when a claimed identity, once assumed, is abandoned or counterfeited. The instrument Jude issues is the epistle itself: a summons that reads the ledger back to the reader before naming the verdict Enoch already filed on their behalf.

The verb running under nearly every case Jude cites — going out, going after, running, wandering, turning aside — is not new vocabulary borrowed to fit the argument. It is the vocabulary Genesis 3 already used for the reader's first departure from an assigned position: taking what was withheld, hiding from the court, being sent out from the garden, and being told that the ground itself was now cursed on account of it. "Because you gave ear to the voice of your wife and took of the fruit of the tree which I said you were not to take, the earth is cursed on your account" (Genesis 3:17), and after that, sent out to till the very ground he was taken from. That is the Bible's own narrative for moving away — not an idea brought in from outside, but the statute the text sets first and then keeps citing. Jude's docket is that same movement recurring: a people carried out of Egypt who still had no faith, angels who left their own kingdom, cities that went after flesh outside their kind, a rebellion that would not stay inside its appointed place. Each case is Genesis 3 running again, and each verdict is Genesis 3's own sentence enforced again.

Egypt — The Enclosure Left Behind

Jude 1:5 recalls how the Lord, having taken a people safely out of Egypt, later sent destruction on those among them who had no faith. Egypt's own name — Mitsrayim, a dual form built on the root for "strait" or "narrow" — names a double confinement before a single Israelite sets foot in the wilderness. The court does not just rescue from bondage; it rescues from a name that means bondage. But deliverance from the enclosure is not the same transaction as the sustained assumption of the new identity: those carried out of the narrow place still perished for want of faith. This is the same mechanism as leaving and cleaving — leaving the old, familiar confinement is only the first half of the statute. Elohim enforces what is occupied afterward, not merely what was escaped.

The Angels — Day One Darkness Before Light

Jude 1:6 describes angels who did not keep to their own kingdom but went out from the place that was theirs, now held in eternal chains under darkness until the great day of judging. This is the vocabulary of Genesis 1:2 — the deep, the darkness, the formless condition prior to the first spoken light.
