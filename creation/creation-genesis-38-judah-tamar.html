The Court & The Creation

Genesis 38 — Judah and Tamar: The Pledge Filed Before the Verdict

Now at that time, Judah went away from his brothers and became the friend of a man of Adullam named Hirah. — Genesis 38:1 (BBE)

Genesis 38 demonstrates the court enforcing what is on file, not what is presented. YHVH, present consciousness, is here named Judah, "praised." He goes down from his brothers, names three sons, holds the third back, and files a promise with the opposite thought held in his mind. Tamar, "palm tree," is the identity meant to be joined, and she is kept from the joining. She reads the time, covers her face, and takes Judah's own seal, cord, and staff as a pledge. When Judah sits as judge and sentences her, the evidence before Elohim, the judges and rulers, is his own filing. The court's instrument in this passage is the pledge filed before the verdict.

Praised Goes Down — Genesis 1:26 Names as Identity Codes

Judah ("praised" — H3063, from yadah/H3034) goes away from his brothers (38:1). The chapter before shows which voice goes: in Genesis 37:26-27 Judah is the one who proposes giving Joseph to the Ishmaelites for a price. Praise leaves the other voices and stands alone. Identity is the creative unit set in Genesis 1:26, and a name discloses the nature of the state being occupied, so the narrative runs according to the meaning. Everything Judah attaches himself to is named. His friend is Hirah ("a noble family" — H2437) of Adullam ("justice of the people" — H5725), the Blue Letter Bible glosses; Strong's gives "splendor" for Hirah and the BDB marks the root dubious. His wife is the daughter of Shua ("wealth" — H7770, Blue Letter Bible) of Canaan (H3667, from kana'/H3665, "humiliated," with "lowland" in the outline). Praise goes down into a lowland, takes a wife from wealth, and keeps a noble friend. The court reads the names, and the chapter that follows is the enforcement.

Watchful and Strong — Genesis Day Three After Its Kind

Three sons follow, each named for the state it carries. Er ("watchful" — H6147, from H5782) does evil in the eyes of the Lord and is put to death (38:7). The text names no deed, so none is added here. Onan ("strong" — H209) is told to "get offspring for your brother" (38:8), sees "that the offspring would not be his," and lets his seed go on to the earth (38:9, BBE). The Hebrew behind "offspring" is zera (H2233), the Genesis 1:11 word, where seed gives back after its kind. The BBE reads "offspring" in 38:8 and 38:9, which hides the Genesis word. Genesis 4:25 shows what a seed raised in place of a dead brother looks like: Seth, in place of Abel. Onan holds the seed back from the one it is for, and the earth receives it with nothing to give back. Both sons are put to death in the same words (38:7, 38:10). The statute is not suspended. A seed not planted for its kind returns nothing.

The Request and the Widow — Genesis 2:24 Leave and Cleave Reversed

Judah takes a wife for Er named Tamar ("palm tree" — H8559, the same as H8558, from a root meaning "to be erect") (38:6). After the two deaths he says to her, "Go back to your father's house and keep yourself as a widow till my son Shelah becomes a man" (38:11, BBE). The leave-and-cleave statute of Genesis 2:24 has the man leave the father and be joined to the wife. Here the wife is sent back to the father's house and kept from the joining, and the statute runs backwards. The same verse records what Judah is actually holding: "he had in his mind the thought that death might come to him as it had come to his brothers." The third son is Shelah ("request" — H7956), born at Chezib ("falsified" — H3580, from kazab/H3576, "to lie") (38:5). The Request is born in a place named for what is false. A promise is filed on the outside and a fear on the inside, and the court enforces the file, not the statement. The I AM meant to be joined stays unjoined, and the Request is not given.

The Time Read — Genesis Day Four Lights

Tamar has news that her father-in-law is going up to Timnah to the wool-cutting (38:13). She takes off her widow's clothing, covers herself with her veil, and sits on the road, "for she saw that Shelah was now a man, but she had not been made his wife" (38:14, BBE). She reads the time. The lights of Genesis day four were set to mark times, and what is due is seen when the time is read, not when the one withholding it decides. Then the coverings change: widow's clothing off, veil on (38:14), veil off and widow's clothing on again (38:19). The same identity wears three coverings in turn, each put on by her own hand. The garment is not the filing. What Elohim enforces is what is held and filed under the covering, not the covering presented to the eye.

Two Eyes — Genesis Day Three Ground

She sits "near Enaim on the road to Timnah" (38:14, BBE). The Hebrew reads "in the opening of Enayim" (petach, H6607, "opening, doorway"), which the YLT keeps and the BBE renders "near." Enaim (H5879) is the dual of ayin (H5869), "eye" or "spring": two eyes, two springs. Timnah ("a portion assigned" — H8553, from manah/H4487) is where the road goes. On day three the court separated the dry land and named it earth (Genesis 1:9-10), and ground is where an identity stands. Here the ground names the state before anyone stands on it: a doorway named for the eyes, on a road to what is assigned. The eyes are what is in question. Judah "took her to be a loose woman of the town, because her face was covered" (38:15), and "he had no idea that she was his daughter-in-law" (38:16). YHVH reads the surface and prepares to rule from it.

The Pledge — Genesis Day Six Identity

And he said, What would you have? And she said, Your ring and its cord and the stick in your hand. So he gave them to her and went in to her, and she became with child by him. — Genesis 38:18 (BBE)

Tamar does not ask for goods. Judah offers a young goat from the flock, and she asks what he will give "as a sign till you send it" (38:17). The Hebrew for sign is arabon (H6162), "a pledge," used three times, all in this chapter (38:17, 38:18, 38:20). She asks for his ring, a seal (chothemeth, H2858, from chatham, "to seal"), its cord, and the stick in his hand, matteh (H4294), "staff, branch, tribe," whose first use in the Bible is Genesis 38:18. Exodus 31:2 uses the same word for the tribe of Judah. What she holds is the mark that makes a thing his and the one word that means both his staff and his tribe. These are Day Six identity instruments, issued from the one called Praise and placed in the hand of the identity he has not joined. This is the central mechanic of the passage. The court always moves from filing to enforcement, and the filing here is Judah's own, made by his own hand before the seed is planted. She becomes with child by him, and the seed carries what was filed.

Judah sends the young goat by Hirah to get the pledge back, and the woman "was not there" (38:20). The Hebrew in 38:15 and 38:24 for "loose woman" is zonah (H2181), but the men of the place answer in 38:21-22 with qedeshah (H6948), "a female devotee" in Strong's and "the separated one" in the YLT, and the BBE uses one phrase for both. Judah goes looking for one name and is answered with the other. His ruling is "Let her keep the things, so that we may not be shamed" (38:23). The pledge stays on file with her.

The Sentence — Sin as Jurisdictional Error

About three months later word comes to Judah that Tamar "had been acting like a loose woman and was with child," and he says, "Take her out and let her be burned" (38:24, BBE). This is sin as jurisdictional error: a ruling made from the visible state while the true evidence stays out of sight. Judah is petitioner and judge at once, sentencing the identity he left unjoined, and the filing he rules against is one he made himself. The court has not yet been shown what it holds. Elohim does not enforce the sentence Judah renders. It enforces what is on file.

The Question Put Back — Genesis 3 The Court Asks

Then Judah said openly that they were his, and said, She is more upright than I am, for I did not give her to Shelah my son. And he had no more connection with her. — Genesis 38:26 (BBE)

She does not argue. While she is being taken out she sends word: "say then, whose are this ring and this cord and this stick?" (38:25, BBE). In Genesis 3:9-11 the court asks the questions and the man answers. Here the identity on trial puts the question to the judge, with the judge's own filing as the evidence. The Hebrew is hakker-na, "recognise, please" (nakar, H5234), the identical words the brothers used on Jacob with Joseph's coat in 37:32. The question the brothers put to Jacob over Joseph's coat comes back to Judah, one of those brothers. Then Judah "said openly," with the same verb, that the pledge was his, and rules: tsadaq (H6663), "more upright than I am." It is a word of forty-one uses, and this is the first. The second is Genesis 44:16, and it is Judah again. He states what was actually filed. Not "she is a harlot," but "I did not give her to Shelah." This is repentance as the framework defines it: the false filing amended, and the I AM shifted back to the blueprint. The word for the pledge has the same source. Arabon comes from arab (H6148, exchange), and its first uses are Judah's own: "I myself will be surety" in 43:9, and 44:32. The one who left a pledge in Tamar's hand becomes the pledge for the boy.

Breach and Rising — Genesis 3:15 Two Seeds

When the time comes, "there were two children in her body" (38:27, BBE). One puts out a hand, the woman ties a red thread on it and says, "This one came out first." He takes his hand back, and his brother comes first: "What an opening you have made for yourself! So he was named Perez" (38:29). Perez ("breach" — H6557, the same as H6556, "a break") breaks through ahead of the one marked. Zerah ("rising" — H2226, Blue Letter Bible) comes out second with the thread on his hand (38:30). The Hebrew root yatsa, "come out," runs through "taken out" in 38:25 and "came out" in 38:29-30, so the woman who was brought out to be burned is the one out of whom two come out. Two seeds in one woman's body recall the shape of Genesis 3:15, seed against seed, an echo and not a word match. The sentence ends in a breach and a rising. Elohim enforces what the pledge declared, after its kind. The vocabulary was set on the days of creation. Genesis 38 runs every thread.
