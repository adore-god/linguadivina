The Court & The Creation

Ecclesiastes 9:1-6 — The Court Keeps the Verdict Off the Record

All this I took to heart, and my heart saw it all: that the upright and the wise and their works are in the hand of God; and men may not be certain if it will be love or hate; all is to no purpose before them. — Ecclesiastes 9:1

Ecclesiastes 9:1-6 continues directly from chapter 8, where the reader was already holding a ruling steady against noise from within. Now the passage removes the one thing that noise usually still leans on: outward proof. Verse 1 states it plainly — "men may not be certain if it will be love or hate." Circumstance gives no sign either way. Verses 2 through 6 trace one fate falling on every pair the reader might use as evidence, until the whole question — was the ruling good or bad — is removed from anything visible under the sun. The court's instrument here is concealment. Not withheld enforcement, as in chapter 8, but a withheld sign: the one thing that would let the reader read the verdict straight off the outward record.

No Sign Discloses It — Genesis 1:31 The Hidden Verdict

"In the hand of God" names custody, not disclosure. Genesis 1:31 shows what that custody holds:

And God saw everything which he had made and it was very good. — Genesis 1:31

This is the verdict the internal court reaches and holds as settled — a ruling that belongs to Elohim, the judicial structure, not to YHVH, the present, asking awareness. Same reader, different faculty. The court does not report its findings up to the part still doing the asking. Ecclesiastes 9:1 names this exact split. "Their works are in the hand of God" places the ruling in the court's keeping. "Men may not be certain if it will be love or hate" names the plain fact: the conscious, present-tense part of the reader — the part capable of doubt in the first place — was never given clearance to read the file. The ruling is real. It is complete and settled. It is simply not addressed to the part of the reader still asking after it.

One Event to All — Genesis 3:22 The Shared Vocabulary of Good and Evil

Because to all there is one event, to the upright man and to the evil, to the clean and to the unclean, to him who makes an offering and to him who makes no offering; as is the good so is the sinner; he who takes an oath is as he who has fear of it. This is evil in all things which are done under the sun: that there is one fate for all, and the hearts of the sons of men are full of evil; while they have life their hearts are foolish, and after that — to the dead. — Ecclesiastes 9:2–3

Every pair Ecclesiastes 9:2 lines up — upright and evil, clean and unclean, one who makes an offering and one who does not, good and sinner — draws on one shared vocabulary. Genesis 3:22 is where that vocabulary enters the record:

And the Lord God said, Now the man has become like one of us, having knowledge of good and evil; and now if he puts out his hand and takes of the fruit of the tree of life, he will go on living for ever. — Genesis 3:22

"Good and evil" became the operating terms for the whole internal plurality once that category entered the record — for Elohim, the many judging voices, not for one voice alone. Ecclesiastes 9:3's "hearts of the sons of men are full of evil" is not a claim about people outside the reader. It names that same plurality, now that it has inherited the good/evil vocabulary — the full bench, not a single judge on it. The court does not treat these outcomes differently. One event falls on the reader's self-judgment as upright exactly as it falls on the reader's self-judgment as sinner, because Genesis 3:22 fixed the category itself, not which side of it the reader currently stands on. Knowing good and evil never produced different weather depending on which judge on the bench is speaking. It produced the same weather — and gave the bench a vocabulary to keep arguing in.

Joined to the Living — Genesis 3:20 Mother of All Living

For him who is joined to all the living there is hope; a living dog is better than a dead lion. — Ecclesiastes 9:4

"Joined to all the living" — kol ha-chayyim — uses the same Hebrew root, chai, as Genesis 3:20:

And the man gave his wife the name of Eve because she was the mother of all who have life. — Genesis 3:20

"Mother of all who have life" is em kol chai in the Hebrew — the same chai. The name itself, Eve ("life-giver" — H2332), carries this reason built in. Within this cleaving, the wife is the assumed I AM, the state taken up as one's own. Naming her Life is the reader declaring, over its own newly occupied identity, that this is where continuation comes from. That naming happens in the very next verse after Genesis 3:19 names dust as the far edge of the ground the reader was taken from. Death is announced, and in the same breath, life is named as the defining mark of the state now held. Ecclesiastes 9:4 makes the identical move inside the reader. Hope does not belong to a grander identity once occupied — the lion, however noble, now dead, a former self no longer lived in. It belongs to remaining joined to whatever identity is presently alive and held — the dog, however modest. This hope has nothing to do with rank. It has to do with which side of chai and the dust the reader is currently standing on — the same division Genesis 3:20 fixes as a name in the very breath Genesis 3:19 fixes as a sentence.

No Memory, No Portion — Genesis 3:19 Dust and the End of the Portion Under the Sun

The living are conscious that death will come to them, but the dead are not conscious of anything, and they no longer have a reward, because there is no memory of them. Their love and their hate and their envy are now ended; and they have no longer a part for ever in anything which is done under the sun. — Ecclesiastes 9:5–6

Genesis 3:19 names the far edge of the ground the reader was taken from:

With the hard work of your hands you will get your bread till you go back to the earth from which you were taken: for dust you are and to the dust you will go back. — Genesis 3:19

"Dust you are and to the dust you will go back" is the boundary Ecclesiastes 9:5 now describes from the inside — no consciousness, no reward, no memory. This is the same ground the provision of Genesis 1:29 belongs to, and the same ground Ecclesiastes 8:15 belongs to:

So I gave praise to joy, because there is nothing better for a man to do under the sun than to take meat and drink and be happy; for that will be with him in his work all the days of his life which God gives him under the sun. — Ecclesiastes 8:15

Both hold a portion good only on this side of the dust line. "No longer a part for ever in anything which is done under the sun" does not describe a punishment added on top of death. It describes the edge of the category itself. Under the sun was always the name for the living side of Genesis 3:19's sentence, never a permanent estate. Love, hatred, and envy — the very noise Ecclesiastes 8 spent a chapter tracking — are named here as ending at that same edge. The struggle to hold a ruling steady against internal voices was always a struggle bounded by dust on one side.

The Answer Already Given — Ecclesiastes 9:7

Come, take your bread with joy, and your wine with a glad heart. God has taken pleasure in your works. — Ecclesiastes 9:7

The very next line answers the six verses before it — and answers by overturning their own premise. "God has taken pleasure in your works" states, as settled fact, the very verdict Ecclesiastes 9:1 said no one could know. The reader is not meant to resolve this by more searching. It is the same instruction Ecclesiastes 8:15 already gave, now restated as fact rather than recommendation. The hidden verdict of verse 1, the one event of verses 2-3, the fragile hope of verse 4, and the dust-bound portion of verses 5-6 are not overturned by verse 7. They are simply not the last word. The court that withheld the sign through verses 1-6 supplies one anyway in verse 7 — on its own timing, not the reader's. The vocabulary was set on the days of creation. Ecclesiastes 9 runs every thread.
